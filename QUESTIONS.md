# Questions to the public

Where the Studiolo project is stuck, at rules revision 14 (27 November 2026). For players of tarot games, historians of card games, librarians, mathematicians and programmers.

We restore the rules of the tarot games of a Turin rule book of 1787 and teach a program to play them. A rule stands in a game file with its source, its page and a mark: printed in the book, our reading, or our decision where the sources are silent. This page gathers the places where reading has taken us as far as it can. Each needs something we do not have: a book, a table where the game is alive, a calculation, a better program.

You do not have to answer a whole question. A page number, a memory of how your table does it, a doubt about the way we put the question: each of these helps. The numbers in square brackets are the numbers of the full list, [`rulesets/OPEN_QUESTIONS.md`](rulesets/OPEN_QUESTIONS.md), where every question has its sources and its other reading.

## 1. Two books we are looking for

**Carlo O..., «Regole inalterabili per tutti i giuochi di tarocco detti di commercio».** Torino: Stamperia Reale, 1830, in-16, 40 pages. The record is in A. Lensi, *Bibliografia italiana di giuochi di carte*, Firenze, 1892, no. 155: «Contiene le regole di varii giuochi di tarocco». We have not read it. Michael Dummett describes it as a collection of laws that takes the games as known (*The Game of Tarot*, 1980, p. 258); his account of it (pp. 258-270) is how we know its rules. One copy is known to us: Oxford, Bodleian Library, shelfmark Jessel f.937. Twelve of our fifteen open questions name this book as the document that could close them.

**«Almanacco per l'anno 1831 contenente le regole inalterabili per tutti i giuochi di tarocco detti di commercio».** Torino: Gaetano Balbino, 40 pages. We know it from one line in a list of Franco Pratesi («Documents on Playing Cards in Piedmont», 1994); the line names no library.

If you can read either book in a library, know of another copy, or know a later book that reprints these rules, tell us. We use only copies that may lawfully be read and shared.

## 2. How does your table rule this?

Six places where the books leave two readings or say nothing. For each we say what the pages print, what stands in our files now, and what we ask. An answer is a source with its page, a living custom (where, with how many players), or an argument why one reading is right.

### Who speaks next after a raise in the auction? [35]

In Permesso and Chiamare il Re an auction comes before the play: the player who names fewer cards to exchange outbids the others. Who speaks next after a raise?

The book of 1787 (pp. 62-63) gives a dialogue at a table of three. The first asks «Permesso?». His neighbour «dirà subito: A due». The first answers «A due anch'io» or gives way. The third player is not named once. The page allows two readings: the turn goes straight back to the player who was outbid, or the book simply left out the pass of the third.

The Turin book of 1846 prints the same example with the third player in it: «un altro ancora dirà: ad una» (part I, p. 42). He speaks before the turn comes back to the first, so the bidding goes round. In Chiamare il Re, read to the letter, the book of 1787 (p. 82) gives the turn back to the dealer, «tu o lo lascierai andare, o risponderai», and says nothing of the other two; the book of 1846 (p. 39) repeats it.

*What stands:* the bidding goes round the table, our reading.

*We ask:* how did they bid in Turin in 1787? Did the custom change by 1846, or did the book of 1787 shorten the dialogue? And how is it done with four, five or seven players?

Sources: Torino 1787, pp. 62-63, 82; Torino [1846], part I, pp. 39, 42; Dummett 1980, p. 265.

### What does a player with no trick lose? [1, 2]

Such a player still holds his discard and, sometimes, the Fool. In Baronetti the book of 1787 says of the Fool: «non sarò io obbligato a dartelo mai … e poi metterlo ne' miei punti» (p. 50). Venticinque for two sends the reader to that place (p. 54), and Mitigati to Venticinque (p. 87). Rule 10 (p. 21) tells the players to agree before the deal whether the «vola» is lost when a player with no trick holds the Fool or the discard.

The Piedmontese «Prammatica» of 1846 says otherwise of the Fool: «chi non potesse coprirlo, alla fine della mano è obbligato di cederlo alla parte vincente» (p. 6). That such a player loses his discard, the Turin books print for one game only: Mitigati for two in a later form, «collo scarto compito» (Prammatica, pp. 24-25). The records of the Piedmontese game in the twentieth century that Dummett gives differ from town to town.

*What stands:* in the games of the book of 1787 the player keeps both, our reading; the rules of 1846 stand beside it as the other reading. (In Venticinque for three the Fool goes to the winning side: that is the printed rule of that game, Torino [1846], part I, pp. 33-34.)

*We ask:* did the custom change in Turin between 1787 and 1846? Or is «mai» on p. 50 said of the play alone, and the Fool was given up at the end of the hand even then? And what of the discard in the games where nothing is printed about it?

Sources: Torino 1787, pp. 21, 50, 54, 87; Prammatica [1846], pp. 6, 24-25; Dummett 1980, pp. 259, 276-277.

### A false «fuora»: what happens next? [25, 11]

In Sedici and Trent'uno the winner is the first to declare «fuora»: that he has the points of the game, 16 or 31. The player declares it himself, and the book tells him to count in silence, his own points and the other player's: «tacitamente conti i tuoi punti» (pp. 39-40, rule 42). For a false declaration at Sedici the book names the penalty: a double payment if the claimant has fewer than eight points, a single one if he has more (p. 39); at exactly eight we read it as single. What becomes of the hand after the payment, it does not say.

The general rule on penalties (p. 20, rule 9) says that after a penalty is paid the cards are not dealt again, «ma seguirassi questa sull'istesso piede»: the same game goes on. Whether this covers a false «fuora», the page does not say. In «À Fora» of Annecy in Savoy, a game close to Trent'uno, a player who declares wrongly loses the game. In the Turin book of 1846 the winner is the first to reach the points, not the first to declare (Prammatica, p. 12).

Of Trent'uno the book of 1787 says that it is played by the rules of Sedici, only to 31 (pp. 45-46). The line below which the payment is double is not printed for it. Dummett gives it as a guess: «probably 15 points» (p. 269).

*What stands:* the claimant pays and the game goes on, our reading; at Trent'uno he pays double below 16 points, our decision.

*We ask:* after a false «fuora», did the hand go on or end? And below which score did a wrong claimant at Trent'uno pay double?

Sources: Torino 1787, pp. 20, 39-40, 45-46; Prammatica [1846], p. 12; Dummett 1980, p. 269; the rules of Annecy as published by Depaulis, 1984, p. 123.

### Does the example of the book add up? [32]

In Venticinque for two the book works an example: one player has 27 points, the other 28 and the Fool, and after the card given for the Fool the score is level. The cards are counted there in pairs, and a plain card gives half a point. The dealer puts away three cards, and other places of the same book point to the discard being counted for him (pp. 21, 54-55).

But then 53 cards are in the count, and with an odd number of cards the score cannot be level: one player has a whole number, the other a number and a half. If the discard is counted for nobody, 50 cards are in the count and a level score can happen. But then, by our calculation, the two must hold 27½ and 28½ before the card is given, not 27 and 28.

One more number. The Turin book of 1846 has a game for two with a dead hand, played «a venticinque carte fra due persone e tre scarti» (part I, p. 47). It names 46 points to win, the cards counted in pairs (p. 49). The whole pack counted so holds 91 points. So 46 is a majority only if all 78 cards are counted, the dealer's discard among them. It is a later book and another game, where the 25 cards of the stock count too: an indirect witness, no more.

*What stands:* the discard counts for the dealer, 53 cards, our reading; the count of 50 cards is kept as the other reading.

*We ask:* how did they count, 53 cards or 50? How are the printed «27» and «28» to be read? Or is there an error in the book, and then where: in the example or in the rule?

Sources: Torino 1787, pp. 21, 52-55; Torino [1846], part I, pp. 47, 49.

### Chiamare il Re: what is paid, and who may bid after the dealer? [8, 29]

In Chiamare il Re the dealer calls a King, and the player who holds it is his partner. The book of 1787 says only that they pay «secondo il convenuto», as agreed (p. 83), and names no figure. Dummett (1980, pp. 268-269) proposes payments and says himself that the payment for the called King is a conjecture.

The dealer speaks first. As we read pp. 80 and 82 of the book, his pass ends the auction and he calls a King; the others may only overcall a bid of the dealer or match it («Ad una vado io»). The other reading: after the dealer passes, the others may still bid.

*What stands:* a stake by agreement, as printed; the dealer's pass ends the auction, our reading.

*We ask:* what was paid at a table where a King was called? And could the others bid once the dealer had passed?

Sources: Torino 1787, pp. 80-84; Dummett 1980, pp. 268-269.

### Meno: who wins and who pays when the fewest points are level? [34]

Of Meno the book prints: «sempre vince colui, che di tutti gli altri compagni fa meno punti» (Torino 1787, p. 78). Who pays, and what happens when two are level, the page does not say.

*What stands:* the player with the most points pays the winner, our reading after Torino [1846], part I, p. 32; players level on the fewest points all win, our decision. At seven players, whatever the deal, several players take no trick at all, and they share the win at zero.

*We ask:* at a table that plays for the fewest points, who pays whom, and what do you do when two are level?

Sources: Torino 1787, p. 78; Torino [1846], part I, p. 32.

## 3. Play and code

These wait for a calculation or a program, not for a document. You do not need to know the old games: you need Node 24 and this repository. [`docs/ENGINE.md`](docs/ENGINE.md) tells how to run the engine in ten minutes; [`problems/`](problems/) has the cards, each with what we know, what would count as an answer and the command to start.

**How do you tell who plays better?** In these games the deal decides much. Two players of nearly equal strength, people or programs, cannot be told apart on a hundred deals: luck drowns skill. The bot lab measures in two ways, both described in [`docs/BOT.md`](docs/BOT.md): the plain one, and a cyclic one where every deal is played once from every seat and gives one observation. The second narrows the interval, and a small difference still needs thousands of games. We ask for a way to compare two players of a game with hidden cards and much luck that is quick and fair, or for an argument why it cannot be quicker. And what is it right to call strength in such a game?

**[001. A stronger opponent](problems/001-stronger-opponent/TASK.md).** Our bot plays a decent game and does not get stronger, however long it thinks. Where does skill hide in games where the deal decides so much, and how would you build an opponent that a good player can climb to?

**[002. Bidding at Permesso](problems/002-bidding-at-permesso/TASK.md).** The bots win most of the contracts they take. A bot that bids more cautiously earns no more, and at three seats it earns less; earlier short runs with bolder bids showed no clear gain either. What is a good bid here, and how do you tell that a player bids too little or too much?

**[003. Meno, the short form for three](problems/003-meno-short-form-for-three/TASK.md)** was closed by revision 14. Many of its hands ended with several winners, and the card asked whether that was the game or our misreading. It was our reading of the count: with a plain card worth 1 the share fell from 44 % to 21.5 % of 200 hands. The card keeps its numbers.

**A defect.** If the engine scores a hand wrongly, plays a card the rules forbid, or a file quotes a page that says something else, open an issue. Give the game, the seed or the deal, and the page.

## 4. How to answer

- Open a [discussion](https://github.com/eugenetsaplin/studiolo/discussions) for a source, a reading, a custom of your table, a calculation. Open an issue for an error in a file.
- Any form will do: two paragraphs, a page number, a calculation, a program, a question back. Write in English, Italian, French or Russian.
- [`CONTRIBUTING.md`](CONTRIBUTING.md) says what happens next.

A rule that stands in our files changes for one of three reasons: a new document, the argument of a specialist, or a defect met by a live player at the table. We read every answer with these three in mind.
